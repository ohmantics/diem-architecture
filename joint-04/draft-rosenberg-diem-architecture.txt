                      Digital Emblems Architecture
                draft-rosenberg-diem-architecture-latest

Abstract

   This document describes an architecture for digital emblems:
   authenticated, typed statements about assets, made by the holder of a
   domain name and published in the DNS.  It defines the roles involved,
   the components that connect them, and how trust is anchored, and it
   explains how individual use cases are accommodated by profiles.  It
   leaves wire formats and protocol details to other documents.

1.  Introduction

   Physical emblems and markings tell people how to treat what they
   mark: a red cross on a hospital roof, a seal on a diplomatic bag, a
   label on a shipment of dangerous goods.  Their digital counterparts
   must do the same for assets that people and software encounter over a
   network, or identify by a name or code: a service, a device, a
   container, a person on duty.  [DIEM-REQS] sets out the use cases and
   the requirements they share.

   A digital emblem, as this architecture describes it, is a statement
   about an asset made by the holder of a domain name.  The holder
   publishes the emblem in the DNS.  A validator finds it with an
   ordinary DNS query and checks it with DNSSEC [RFC4033], which tells
   the validator which domain name holder made the statement and that
   the statement has not been altered.  Where a use case requires it, a
   separate authorizing entity confirms, in its own part of the DNS,
   that the issuer is entitled to make the statement.  What the
   statement means is set by a profile, which describes the content of
   emblems for one or more use cases.

   This document describes the roles involved, the components that
   connect them, how trust is anchored, and how profiles accommodate
   individual use cases.  It is the basis for protocol documents that
   will specify encodings, record types, and procedures, and for profile
   documents that will specify the content of emblems for particular use
   cases.  It does not itself specify any of these.

2.  Terminology

   This document uses the terms asset, digital emblem (or emblem), and
   validation as [DIEM-REQS] defines them, and DNS terms as [RFC9499]
   defines them.  It uses the term profile for what [DIEM-REQS] calls a
   digital emblem profile, with the narrower scope described in
   Section 8.  It refines the [DIEM-REQS] terms emblem issuer,
   authorizing entity, and validator by what each produces and consumes,
   and adds the other terms below.

   *Emblem issuer (issuer)*:  Produces emblems about its assets, at
      names in a DNS zone it controls.  Consumes authorizations, where
      its use case requires them.

   *Emblem publisher*:  Serves the issuer's emblems in the DNS.  It may
      be the issuer itself or a provider to which the issuer delegates
      part of its namespace.

   *Authorizing entity*:  Produces authorizations, in a DNS zone it
      controls.

   *Authorization*:  A statement by an authorizing entity that an issuer
      may issue emblems of a stated scope (for example, a profile, the
      purposes it may claim, and a period).

   *Validator*:  Consumes emblems, authorizations, and the DNSSEC data
      that authenticates them.  Produces a result: valid, invalid, or
      indeterminate.

   *Bundle*:  An emblem together with the DNSSEC records needed to
      validate it, carried so that it can be validated without querying
      the DNS.

3.  Design Principles

   The architecture follows these principles.  Each is a choice among
   alternatives that have been discussed in the working group, and the
   rest of the document depends on them.

   *Use the DNS*:  Emblems are discovered, distributed, and
      authenticated with the DNS wherever it can do the job.  If a
      profile ends up delivering data outside the DNS, that data is
      referred to, and its integrity pinned, from a DNSSEC-signed
      record.

   *The assertion belongs to the name holder*:  An emblem is a statement
      made by whoever controls a domain name: an organization, a brand,
      an authority.  Trust follows the namespace, through DNSSEC
      [RFC4033], rather than through any of the many certification
      authorities that a trust store contains.  A validator therefore
      learns who is making the statement, not only that some
      certification authority vouched for a key.

   *One validation model*:  Establishing that an emblem is authentic,
      authorized, and current works the same way for every use case.  A
      validator should not need software specific to a use case to do
      it, so that general-purpose software (including web browsers) can
      validate and display emblems.

   *Profiles describe content, not protocol*:  Use cases differ in what
      their emblems say, not in how emblems are found or trusted.  A
      profile is mainly a schema.  Where a use case needs a mechanism,
      the mechanism belongs in the common architecture, so that every
      use case can rely on it.

   *Undetectable validation*:  Checking an emblem should reveal as
      little as possible about who is checking, and to whom, because in
      several use cases the pattern of checks is itself sensitive.

4.  Domain Model

   An emblem is a statement by an issuer about an asset, its subject.
   The subject may be a single item or a composite (a consignment, a
   convoy) whose parts carry emblems of their own.  The subject is the
   thing the statement is about: for a hospital's records service, the
   service, not the host it runs on; for a diplomatic bag, the bag, not
   the courier carrying it.  An emblem identifies its subject, and may
   carry further identifiers (a seal number, a serial number) that a
   validator can compare with what is in front of it.

   An emblem makes assertions about its subject.  Some describe it (what
   it is, who operates it, what it contains).  Some ask something of
   whoever reads the emblem: not to attack, not to open, to handle with
   care, to report.  The architecture calls these requests treatment.
   An emblem may cite the law, agreement, or standard on which its
   assertions rest, and may name a visual mark that a person can be
   shown alongside the result.

   An emblem states the period during which its assertions hold.  It can
   be withdrawn before the period ends, and it can be replaced.  This
   period is part of what the issuer asserts; it is distinct from how
   long the DNS may cache the emblem and from how long its signatures
   can be verified (Section 6.6).

   In some use cases anyone may issue an emblem about their own asset,
   and a validator needs to know only who the issuer is.  In others the
   issuer must be authorized: a ministry of health authorizes a hospital
   to use a protective emblem, or a national plant protection
   organization authorizes a producer to mark wood packaging [ISPM15].
   More than one authorizing entity may authorize the same issuer,
   including entities whose claims compete (two states that both claim
   the territory where a hospital stands), and a validator decides which
   it recognizes.

   One asset may carry emblems under several profiles at once: a
   shipment may carry customs data, dangerous goods data, and recycling
   data, each described by a different profile and read by a different
   validator.  An emblem may also refer to other emblems (a consignment
   to the products it contains).  Each referenced emblem remains the
   statement of its own issuer and is validated on its own; a reference
   may pin the version it refers to.

   Some of what an emblem says may be meant for some validators only:
   the value of a shipment for customs, the identity of a courier for
   the receiving state.

   An emblem does not create the status it signals.  Protection under
   international humanitarian law, for example, exists in law whether or
   not an emblem is displayed, so there the absence of an emblem does
   not mean the absence of protection.  In other use cases absence does
   carry meaning (wood packaging without a compliance mark may be
   refused entry), and each profile says what absence means for it.  The
   architecture does not decide who is entitled to issue or authorize
   emblems; that is for the frameworks behind each use case.  It does
   not enforce treatment; it lets a validator learn, reliably, what is
   asked and by whom.

5.  Roles and Interactions

   Figure 1 shows the roles and what passes between them.

             v                                   v

             | emblem                            | authorization
             | (queried, or                      | (queried, or
             |  carried in                       |  carried with
             |  a bundle)                        |  the emblem)
             |        +---------------+          |
             +------->|   Validator   |<---------+
                      +-------+-------+
                              | result: who asserts what
                              v
                      +---------------+
                      | Person or     |
                      | system acting |
                      | on it         |
                      +---------------+

   The issuer wants its assertions about its asset to be found and
   accepted as authentic by those they are meant for, and not read by
   others where they are confidential.  The authorizing entity wants its
   authorization to be relied on for the scope it granted and no
   further, and to be able to end it.  The validator wants to learn,
   reliably and without revealing more than necessary about itself, what
   is asserted about an asset and by whom.  The emblem publisher serves
   records; it need not be trusted for their integrity, because what it
   serves is signed by the issuer (unless the issuer has it sign on its
   behalf), although it can see queries and withhold answers.  The
   person or system acting on the result decides what to do; the
   architecture informs that decision but does not make it.

   These are roles, not organizations.  An issuer may run its own emblem
   publisher or delegate that to a provider.  An authorizing entity may
   also be the issuer of its own emblems.  A validator may be a person
   with a handheld device, a program scanning a network, or a service
   acting for others.

   Validation happens in three patterns, which a single validator may
   combine:

   *Online*:  The validator queries the DNS for the emblem and any
      authorization it needs, through a resolver of its choice.

   *Offline*:  The validator receives a bundle (from a suitable storage
      medium such as a tag, a code, a file, or other mechanism) and
      validates it without network access, against trust anchors it
      already holds.

   *Undetectable*:  The validator obtains what it needs in a way that
      does not reveal its interest to the issuer or the authorizing
      entity, for example because the authorization is carried with the
      emblem and the query reaches the emblem publisher only through a
      shared resolver.

6.  Components

   Each component below is described by the properties it must provide.
   Encodings, record types, and procedures are left to protocol
   documents.

6.1.  Emblem Information Model

   An emblem is a dictionary of metadata about its subject: a set of
   entries, each pairing a name with a typed value.  Profiles are
   schemas for that dictionary (Section 8).  The architecture defines
   only what every emblem must be able to carry:

   *  the identifiers of its subject;

   *  the profile or profiles it follows;

   *  the period during which its assertions hold;

   *  references to related emblems; and

   *  values that are confidential to some validators (Section 6.5).

   Everything else, such as treatment or the legal basis that an emblem
   under international humanitarian law cites, is defined by profiles.
   The issuer is not an entry, because it is established by the domain
   name at which the emblem is published.

   Values are typed, and where possible drawn from types and code lists
   shared across profiles, so that a validator without software specific
   to a profile can still authenticate an emblem and present its content
   in a generic form.

   [DIEM-REQS] lets a profile require validation of all of an emblem's
   data or only a defined subset, so that frequently changing data need
   not be re-signed.  The information model therefore distinguishes data
   that must be validated from data that a profile allows to go
   unvalidated, and a validator never presents the latter as validated.

6.2.  Discovery and Distribution

   Emblems are published at names in the issuer's namespace.  An issuer
   may place them beneath an underscored label [RFC8552], which lets it
   delegate that label to an authoritative server that serves only
   emblems (an emblem publisher) while the rest of its zone stays where
   it is.

   An asset that has a domain name, such as a network service, is
   identified by that name, as the charter requires [CHARTER].  An asset
   without one (a bag, a pallet, a person) is given a name in the
   issuer's namespace, which the asset carries (printed, or stored in a
   tag) or which is otherwise made known to the validator.  A validator
   retrieves the emblems at that name with an ordinary DNS query.
   Emblems under several profiles can share a name, and a validator
   selects those whose profiles it understands.

   If a profile ends up delivering data outside the DNS (data too large
   or too dynamic for it), a record in the emblem refers to the data and
   pins its digest (for fixed data) or its signing key (for data that
   changes), as DANE [RFC6698] pins keys for TLS.

   For offline use, an emblem travels as a bundle: its records, any
   authorizations it needs, and the DNSSEC records that chain them to a
   trust anchor.  A bundle can be carried on any suitable medium, and it
   is validated exactly as if its records had been queried.

6.3.  Authorization

   An authorization states that an issuer, named by its domain name, may
   issue emblems within a stated scope.  The scope may be broad (any
   emblem under a given profile, for given purposes, during a given
   period) or narrow (emblems about one named asset), and may bind the
   issuer's keys.  A validator checks that an authorization covers the
   particular emblem it is validating.

   An authorizing entity publishes its authorizations in its own zone,
   at names derived from the issuer's name, so that a validator can look
   them up.  An issuer can also carry an authorization with its emblem,
   so that a validator need not contact the authorizing entity; this
   supports offline and undetectable validation.  Either way, the
   authorizing entity ends an authorization by withdrawing it, and
   carried copies remain usable only until their signatures expire.

   Which authorizing entities a validator recognizes is a matter of
   policy.  A profile may name a default set, but the validator decides.
   Several authorizing entities may authorize one issuer, including
   entities whose claims compete; the validator is given all of them and
   determines which it recognizes.

   Withdrawal and signature expiry are how authorizations account for
   the compromise of key material, as [DIEM-REQS] requires; a profile
   chooses lifetimes short enough for its use case.

6.4.  Validation

   To validate an emblem, a validator establishes that:

   *  it is authentic: its DNSSEC chain validates from a trust anchor
      the validator holds;

   *  it applies: its subject identifiers match the asset in question;

   *  it is current: its asserted period and its signatures are both in
      effect;

   *  it is authorized, where its profile requires: an authorization
      from a recognized authorizing entity covers it; and

   *  it conforms to its profile.

   The result is one of three:

   *Valid*:  Every required check succeeded.

   *Invalid*:  A check failed: a signature did not verify, the emblem
      does not apply to the asset, or no authorization from a recognized
      authorizing entity covers it where one is required.

   *Indeterminate*:  Validation could not be completed: the DNS was
      unreachable, or the profile is unknown.

   An indeterminate result is never presented as invalid, nor as valid.
   A validator that does not know an emblem's profile can still
   establish that the emblem is authentic, applies, and is current, and
   can present what it understands of the content without claiming that
   the emblem conforms to a profile.  This is a further reason for types
   and code lists that many profiles need to be defined in the core
   architecture (Section 8), rather than separately by each profile.
   Whatever the result, the validator learns who asserts what: the
   issuer's domain name, the authorizing entities that authorize it, and
   which of them it recognizes.

6.5.  Confidentiality and Selective Disclosure

   Some values are meant for some validators only.  Approaches include:

   *Encrypted values*:  The emblem carries values encrypted to named
      recipients.  Everyone receives the same signed answer, which can
      be cached and carried offline, and no validator identifies itself;
      but the recipients must be known when the emblem is issued.

   *Disclosed values*:  The emblem carries salted digests of values, and
      whoever accompanies the asset reveals values to a validator, who
      checks them against the digests.

   Both keep a single signed emblem that every requester receives, so
   answers stay cacheable and portable, and no validator has to identify
   itself to the emblem publisher.  Each profile chooses which values to
   protect and how, but the mechanisms are common to all profiles.

6.6.  Lifecycle

   Three periods govern an emblem:

   *  the period its assertions hold, which the issuer states;

   *  how long DNS caches may keep it, set by its time to live; and

   *  how long its signatures can be verified, which bounds how long a
      copy remains usable after the emblem is withdrawn.

   A profile chooses signature lifetimes by weighing prompt withdrawal
   against offline use.  A bundle remains usable only until the earliest
   expiration among all the signatures in its chain, including those of
   parent zones that the issuer does not control, and only for a
   validator with a reliable clock; a similar chain is carried in TLS by
   [RFC9102].

   An issuer replaces an emblem by publishing new records, and withdraws
   it by removing them.  Once cached copies expire, a validator that
   queries receives authenticated denial of existence [RFC4035].

   [DIEM-REQS] requires some emblems to be removable, so that nobody can
   later determine that an emblem was ever applied.  That requires the
   issuer's zone not to reveal its names.  NSEC3 [RFC5155] hashes names
   but does not prevent them from being guessed offline; online signing
   with minimally covering [RFC4470] or compact [RFC9824] denial of
   existence does.  Removal cannot reach copies that validators already
   hold, nor records of past queries kept by resolvers or by collectors
   of DNS traffic.  Conversely, a validator can keep the signed records
   it received as evidence that the emblem was signed for a period that
   included the time it received them, which supports the weakest level
   of proof of presence in [DIEM-REQS].  A profile whose use case
   requires removability states what such retained copies may reveal.

7.  Trust Model

   A validator must be able to establish who made a statement, and that
   it has not changed since.  The architecture anchors this in DNSSEC
   [RFC9364].  An issuer's emblems are signed with the keys of the zone
   that holds them, and those keys are authenticated by a chain of
   signatures from the DNS root, or from another trust anchor the
   validator holds (which is then trusted for every name beneath it),
   down through the parents of that zone.

   What each role must trust follows from this:

   *  The validator trusts its trust anchors, and the operators of the
      zones above the issuer's name not to sign falsely for it.  It does
      not need to trust resolvers, emblem publishers, or the networks
      between them, since DNSSEC lets it check what they deliver.

   *  The validator trusts the issuer for what the issuer asserts about
      its own assets, and, where the profile requires it, an authorizing
      entity it recognizes for the issuer's entitlement.

   *  The issuer and the authorizing entity trust whoever operates their
      zones and holds their signing keys.

   The usual alternative, binding an issuer's key with a certificate
   from a certification authority in the validator's trust store, is not
   adequate for emblems.  A trust store typically contains many
   certification authorities, any of which is technically able to issue
   a certificate for any name, and a certificate binds a key to whatever
   one of them vouched for rather than to an organization the validator
   can recognize.  Trust stores have been subverted in practice: through
   compromised certification authorities [DIGINOTAR], through
   intermediate certification authority keys generated so weakly that
   anyone could recover them [EMUDHRA], and through governments
   requiring users to install roots of their own [KAZAKHSTAN].
   Transparency logs [RFC6962] make misissuance detectable after the
   fact, but do not prevent it.  Revocation needs a separate status
   mechanism, which validators have often been unable to reach or have
   not checked [REVOCATION].

   Anchoring in DNSSEC, and distributing through the DNS, give the
   architecture the following properties.

   *The assertion is the name holder's*:  A validated emblem is the
      statement of whoever holds the domain name, which a validator, or
      the person it serves, can associate with an organization or brand.

   *Subversion is confined*:  Only the operators of the issuer's zone
      and of its parents, and those who can change its registration, can
      sign for the name or redirect it.  This is still trust: a state
      that operates a top-level domain could forge or suppress emblems
      beneath it, and DNSSEC has no counterpart to transparency logs.
      An issuer can weigh this in choosing where its name sits.

   *Absence can be proven*:  DNSSEC can prove that a name or a record
      does not exist [RFC4035].  A validator can therefore distinguish
      the absence of an emblem from an answer that was blocked or
      stripped in transit, and a profile can give absence a meaning
      (Section 4).  A proof of absence says nothing about the past, so
      it does not prevent an issuer from removing an emblem without
      trace ([DIEM-REQS], Removable).

   *Withdrawal is part of publication*:  Withdrawing an emblem means
      removing or replacing its records; copies made earlier stop
      validating when their signatures expire.

   *It works offline*:  An emblem carried in a bundle with its chain of
      signatures can be validated against a trust anchor the validator
      already holds.  The signatures' validity periods bound both how
      long the bundle is usable and how long a withdrawn emblem can
      still be accepted this way (Section 6.6).

   *Checking can be discreet*:  A DNS query goes through a resolver, and
      reaches the issuer's servers only when the resolver has no cached
      answer.  An issuer that wants to observe queries can defeat
      caching, so undetectable validation also relies on carried
      authorizations and bundles (Section 5).

   Emblems and authorizations are both authenticated with DNSSEC.  This
   architecture does not define an alternative root of trust for
   authorization; see [DIEM-REQS], Authorization.  If a profile ends up
   delivering data outside the DNS, that data is bound to the DNS by a
   digest or key published in a signed record, so that the DNSSEC chain
   covers it too.

8.  Profiles

   Because an emblem is a dictionary of metadata (Section 6.1), a
   profile is in essence a schema for that dictionary, for one or more
   use cases, together with the policy for validating emblems that
   follow it.  [DIEM-REQS] also has a profile state how emblems are
   discovered and which validation and authorization mechanisms apply;
   in this architecture, those are choices among the options described
   in Section 6, not new mechanisms.

   A profile has an identifier, and states:

   *  the entries its emblems use, their value types and code lists, and
      which entries are required;

   *  which values are confidential, to whom, and by which approach
      (Section 6.5);

   *  whether authorization is required, the default set of authorizing
      entities, and whether authorizations must be carried with the
      emblem;

   *  which validation patterns it supports (Section 5), with the threat
      models that [DIEM-REQS] requires for undetectable validation and
      removability;

   *  how emblem publishers respond to queries ([DIEM-REQS], Query
      Response);

   *  signature lifetimes, and what absence of an emblem means; and

   *  what a validator should do with each validation result.

   A profile may define new entries, value types, and code lists, and
   may publish additional records at an emblem's name.  It does not
   change how emblems are discovered, their anchoring in DNSSEC, or how
   their authenticity is established.  Where a use case needs a
   mechanism that the architecture lacks, the mechanism should be added
   to the core architecture, so that later profiles share one mechanism
   rather than converging on the same choices only by convention.

9.  Future Work

   Protocol documents are needed for:

   *  the emblem format and its record type;

   *  authorizations;

   *  bundles for offline validation; and

   *  the first profiles.

10.  Security Considerations

   This section follows [RFC3552] in outline; Section 7 describes the
   trust model on which it depends.

   *Forgery*:  Forging an emblem requires the signing key of the
      issuer's zone or of one of its parents.  Validators depend on
      those parents not to sign falsely for the issuer's name.

   *Replay*:  A withdrawn emblem can be replayed until its signatures
      expire (Section 6.6).  A proof of absence can likewise be
      replayed, making a newly published emblem appear absent until the
      proof's signatures expire.

   *Misleading names*:  Authentication shows who the issuer is, not that
      the issuer is who a person expects.  An issuer can choose a name
      that resembles another's.  Validators should present the issuer's
      name in a form people can check, and profiles that need more
      require authorization.

   *Compromised or coerced authorizing entities*:  An authorizing entity
      can authorize an issuer it should not.  A validator chooses which
      authorizing entities it recognizes, and competing authorizations
      are visible to it.

   *Downgrade*:  An attacker can strip signatures or block answers.
      DNSSEC lets a validator detect both, so a validator does not treat
      a missing or unsigned answer as proof that no emblem exists.

   *Misuse as an intrusion detector*:  If checking for an emblem is
      visible to the issuer, an emblem can alert its holder to
      reconnaissance ([DIEM-REQS]).  Undetectable validation (Section 5)
      mitigates this, within the threat model that each profile states
      ([DIEM-REQS]).

   *Denial of service*:  An attacker can make emblem publishers
      unreachable.  The result is indeterminate, not invalid, and
      bundles remain usable.

11.  Privacy Considerations

   This section follows [RFC6973] in outline.

   DNS queries reveal a validator's interest to its resolver, and, for
   the names they serve, to the issuer's servers and those of
   authorizing entities.  A validator limits this by querying through a
   shared resolver, by obtaining authorizations carried with the emblem,
   and by validating bundles offline (Section 5).

   Names can reveal assets.  An issuer avoids names that describe what
   they mark, and keeps its zone from being enumerated (Section 6.6), so
   that a list of protected assets cannot be read from the DNS.

   Emblems can reveal more than their holders intend.  Values meant for
   some validators only are protected as described in Section 6.5.
   Anything carried on an asset, such as a bundle in a tag, can be read
   by anyone with access to the asset.
